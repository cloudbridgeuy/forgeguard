# ForgeGuard — Competitive Teardown

*Version 1.0 — July 2026*
*Companion to the Problem & Product Brief. Purpose: falsify or confirm the brief's differentiation hypothesis against the products closest to it, and explain why none of them surfaced when the founding customer searched for a solution while actively bleeding.*

---

## Method

The brief stated a hypothesis to be falsified: *no existing product makes the tenant hierarchy the privileged spine of the model, unifies entitlements with permissions at the enforcement point, and offers a structural exit hatch.* Each competitor below is measured against that hypothesis plus the brief's adoption conditions: the two form factors (edge proxy and native middleware), the signed header contract, the two-plane policy model with provenance, tenant-based pricing, and the $0–30 / sub-$1,000 price ladder. Findings are as of July 2026; this market is moving fast — several of these products repositioned or relaunched within the last eight months — so this document has a shelf life of roughly two quarters.

The verdict up front, because candor was the assignment: **the hypothesis survives, but narrower than the brief implied.** Nobody combines all three differentiators. But every individual piece of ForgeGuard now exists somewhere in the market, at least in partial form, and two products — one of which we never discussed — are close enough that they change how ForgeGuard must position itself.

## The graph engines: SpiceDB/AuthZed and OpenFGA

SpiceDB is the most mature open-source implementation of Google's Zanzibar model — Apache 2.0, backed by AuthZed's managed offerings, and battle-proven at serious scale: OpenAI runs ChatGPT Enterprise permissions on it, handling tens of billions of relationships. Its distinguishing engineering is consistency: ZedTokens let each check choose between full consistency and fast snapshot reads, solving the stale-cache race the Zanzibar paper calls the New Enemy Problem. OpenFGA is the same architectural species — a CNCF-incubating Zanzibar engine that powers Okta's FGA product.

Measured against the brief: these are *engines*, not products in ForgeGuard's sense. They answer "can subject X do Y to Z?" over a relationship graph, and everything else is the reader's exercise — exactly the exercise that hurt. There is no tenancy spine: multi-tenancy is a modeling convention you build in schema, not a privileged structure the product understands, bills on, or puts in a header. There is no enforcement point: every service still calls a check API, which means every service still contains auth code — the decorator problem with better plumbing. There are no entitlements, no operator UI for feature rollout, no policy-as-code plane with provenance. Self-hosting SpiceDB is real but heavy: it wants PostgreSQL or CockroachDB and Kubernetes operational maturity.

What to respect and steal: the consistency design. ForgeGuard's grants-as-live-data decision will eventually meet the New Enemy Problem, and ZedTokens are the prior art. What not to fear: neither project is moving toward ForgeGuard's territory of opinionated tenancy, enforcement, or entitlements — their ambition points at scale, not shape.

## The policy engines: Cerbos, OPA, AWS Verified Permissions

Cerbos is the product that currently owns the position ForgeGuard's exit-hatch requirement points at. The policy decision point is fully open source under Apache 2.0, written in Go, stateless, deployable as a single binary or sidecar with sub-millisecond evaluation, with policies in YAML plus CEL expressions and a genuinely GitOps-native workflow. The commercial layer, Cerbos Hub, sells centralized policy management with an entry tier around $25/month. That is the open-core shape the brief demands, already executed and credible.

What Cerbos lacks is the model. It has no relationship graph — ReBAC requires pairing it with another system — so the org tree, the grant edges, the "view as" scope-down, and the sharing semantics are all outside its world. It has no entitlements plane, no operator UI persona, no header contract; it decides, but each service still asks. Cerbos is a superb *component* of the world the brief describes, not that world. Notably, its 2026 positioning has pivoted hard toward AI-agent authorization — gating which MCP tools an agent may call — which says something about where this whole category thinks the money is.

OPA deserves one paragraph of honesty: it is the CNCF-graduated incumbent, free, general-purpose, and it is what several competitors run under the hood. But Rego's learning curve is notorious, it has no tenancy model, no data plane, and no product surface — and the commercial ecosystem around it was destabilized when Apple acqui-hired the Styra founders, leaving enterprise support in transition. AWS Verified Permissions, familiar territory from ForgeGuard's own history with Cedar, remains a managed policy evaluator: per-request pricing, AWS-captive, no tenancy spine, no enforcement point, no ops persona. Both confirm rather than threaten the brief's gap.

## The developer-first platform: Oso Cloud

Oso's differentiator is Polar, a purpose-built logic language that expresses RBAC, ReBAC, and ABAC in one model rather than forcing a paradigm choice, with strong SDK ergonomics and good documentation. Its most technically interesting feature is Local Authorization: Oso never reads your data but understands your schema, and compiles authorization decisions into SQL fragments your own PostgreSQL executes — an elegant answer to the list-filtering problem ("show me only the rows this user can see") that check-API products handle badly. Pricing is a free developer tier, then roughly $149/month.

Against the brief: Oso made the opposite strategic bet on the exit hatch. It began as an open-source embeddable library and deliberately pivoted to a closed, hosted-first product; the legacy library is deprecated in spirit. There is no enforcement point — the model is SDK check calls, so services keep their auth code. Multi-tenancy and org hierarchies are modeling *patterns* in Polar (their docs walk through them), not privileged product structure. No entitlements plane, no operator persona, no header contract. Oso competes for the developer who wants a smarter check call; ForgeGuard's thesis is that the check call itself, scattered across services, is the disease.

## The full-stack authorization service: Permit.io

Permit.io is the broadest overlap with the brief and must be taken seriously. It offers full-stack authorization as a service: a sidecar PDP (running OPA or Cedar underneath — you choose the engine), a no-code policy UI aimed at non-developers, policy-as-code generated into Git, Terraform support, ReBAC, and explicit multi-tenant features including tenant-specific policies and hierarchical tenant relationships. Its pricing moved aggressively downmarket: a free tier to 1,000 MAUs with no feature blackouts, and a Startup tier around $150/month for 10,000 users, metered on a combination of monthly-active identities and tenant quotas. Its 2026 homepage is a wall of AI-agent security positioning — an MCP gateway that interrogates agents, gates tokens, and carries authorization context downstream.

That last clause deserves attention: "carry that context downstream so the same policy follows the action" is the closest any competitor comes to ForgeGuard's signed header contract. The two-persona insight is also already here — Permit explicitly markets to developers *and* non-technical teams managing permissions through a UI.

Where the brief's model still wins: Permit's multi-tenancy is a feature set layered on general-purpose engines, not an enforced tree spine with stable identity beneath it — there is no equivalent of the FGRN/selector split, no snapshot-versioned decision replay as a product primitive, and provenance across UI-born versus repo-born policy is not the organizing principle. The exit hatch is partial at best: only the data-sync layer (OPAL) is open source; the product is hosted-primary, and the reviewed weakness is precisely that teams needing full control over their authorization infrastructure find the deployment options limited. And the meter is MAUs — softened by tenant quotas, but still the meter the brief's founding customer rage-quits.

## The one we missed: Frontegg

Frontegg never came up in the discovery interview, and it is the single closest product to the brief's *convergence* claim. It is a CIAM platform (authentication included) whose Entitlements Engine explicitly unifies the things the brief says belong together: features, subscription plans, feature flags, and permissions, all evaluated through one `isEntitledTo` query, managed by non-developers in a portal, with tenant-level feature targeting. In 2025 it added API Access Controls: authorization policies configured in the portal and enforced *at the gateway level*, marketed precisely as removing the need to embed security logic in every microservice. A dockerized entitlements agent runs in your network as a sidecar for low-latency decisions. Feature flags with targeting rules are a first-class product surface — this is the LaunchDarkly-absorption thesis, shipped.

So why does the brief survive contact with Frontegg? Four reasons, all structural. First, no developer plane: policy lives in the portal, not in TOML in a repo with PR review — the two-persona convergence runs one-directional, operator-first, with no provenance model and no policy-as-code spine. Second, no model: entitlements are keyed features and plans, not a DAG with a tree spine, grant edges, and stable identity — the "view as" scope-down and document-sharing semantics are outside its vocabulary. Third, total captivity: closed SaaS, CIAM bundle, your identities and policies live in their cloud; it is the anti-exit-hatch. Fourth, it competes as an identity suite, which means its authorization depth is bounded by CIAM economics. Frontegg proves the convergence demand is real — a competitor shipping your thesis is validation — while leaving the developer-plane, model, and sovereignty ground unoccupied.

## The distribution threat: WorkOS FGA

WorkOS matters less for what its authorization product is than for who is holding it. The company reached a $2B valuation in March 2026 with OpenAI, Cursor, Anthropic, and Vercel as customers, and its FGA product was deprecated in late 2025 and relaunched, re-architected, in Q1 2026. The relaunch's data model is a striking piece of independent validation of the brief: a typed resource hierarchy up to five layers deep in which *every resource instance has exactly one parent*, chosen explicitly for predictable inheritance and traversal — the tree spine, arrived at by a well-funded team looking at the same B2B SaaS pain. Their documentation even argues against local-evaluation approaches and against syncing high-cardinality objects into the authorization store, staking out clear architectural opinions. Pricing is notable too: free to ten million checks per month, with the broader platform priced per enterprise connection — organization-shaped pricing, adjacent to the brief's tenant meter.

The gaps: it is a closed hosted service inside a CIAM bundle (captivity again), the hierarchy is capped and rigid where ForgeGuard's spine is reconfigurable with stable FGRN identity underneath, there is no policy-as-code plane, no entitlements/flags convergence, no enforcement point — services call check APIs. But WorkOS has the distribution to define the category's vocabulary for a generation of B2B startups, and its model choices will feel familiar to anyone later evaluating ForgeGuard. That is both a headwind and a free education campaign for the tree-spine idea.

## Verdict: the hypothesis, revised

The brief's differentiation hypothesis survives in this sharpened form. Still unoccupied, as a combination: the enforced tree spine with lateral grants as one evaluated graph; stable identity split from positional selectors (no competitor has an FGRN equivalent — identifiers are either positional or absent); the signed header contract that lets downstream services delete auth code rather than call a check API (Permit's MCP gateway gestures at it; nobody ships it as the product); the two-plane policy model where repo-born and UI-born rules merge with provenance into versioned, replayable snapshots; and the open-source enforcement core with tenant-metered hosted convenience.

Individually occupied, and the brief must stop implying otherwise: gateway-level enforcement plus entitlements plus flags exists (Frontegg); UI-plus-code dual personas with multi-tenant features and sidecar PDPs exist (Permit.io); the open-core exit-hatch business exists (Cerbos); the single-parent hierarchy model now ships from a unicorn (WorkOS); planet-scale relationship graphs are solved open source (SpiceDB). ForgeGuard's claim is the *composition*, and compositions are harder to communicate than features — which leads directly to the last section.

One more candid observation: every single one of these companies repositioned toward AI-agent authorization during 2025–2026 — Permit's agent gateway, Cerbos's MCP tool gating, WorkOS's agent identities, Oso's LLM-context authorization. The instinct that started this project ("simple AAA for agentic-built applications") is now the loudest marketing message in the category. That is validation of the direction and a warning about the noise floor: "authorization for AI agents" is no longer a differentiator, it is table stakes messaging. ForgeGuard's differentiation must live in the model and the sovereignty story, not the agent framing.

## Why none of this surfaced when it mattered

The founding customer searched for a solution during two real projects, found Auth0, Clerk, and Keycloak, judged the field overengineered/expensive/incomplete, and never encountered Permit, Oso, Cerbos, SpiceDB, or Frontegg. That failure is worth dissecting, because it is not a personal failure — it is a structural property of this market, and it will apply to ForgeGuard too.

The first mechanism is vocabulary capture. Searches naturally use the word "auth," and the authentication market — vastly larger, older, and better funded — owns that word's search results, ad auctions, and recommendation threads. Auth0, Clerk, and Keycloak are the answer to "auth solution" the way Kleenex is the answer to "tissue." The authorization products deliberately avoid competing for that word and instead coined their own: *fine-grained authorization*, *FGA*, *ReBAC*, *policy engine*, *PDP*, *externalized authorization*, *Zanzibar*. Their content marketing targets people who already know those terms — the category is discoverable only from inside its own jargon, a bootstrapping problem for any newcomer searching from pain rather than from literature.

The second mechanism is that "AAA" as a search term belongs to a different industry. In networking, AAA means RADIUS and TACACS+ — device and network access control — so searching for a "complete AAA solution" surfaces Cisco documentation and network-access vendors, not application authorization startups. The very framing that made the need precise made it unsearchable.

The third mechanism is category fragmentation mirroring the product fragmentation. The three A's are sold by three markets that do not reference each other: CIAM sells authentication, the FGA niche sells authorization, and observability vendors sell what passes for audit. Entitlements hide in a fourth aisle labeled *feature management*, where LaunchDarkly defined the category so completely that "roll out a feature per tenant" does not even read as an authorization query. A buyer bleeding from all three A's at once finds no shelf where they sit together — which is, recursively, the product thesis restated as a marketing problem.

The fourth mechanism is timing and size. WorkOS FGA in its current form did not exist during those projects (relaunched Q1 2026). Permit, Oso, and Cerbos are small companies whose reach is conference talks, dev.to posts, and each other's comparison pages — visible to people browsing the authorization literature, invisible to someone with two weeks to ship an auth layer.

The strategic lesson cuts both ways and belongs in ForgeGuard's plan explicitly. As threat: a superior model does not get discovered; ForgeGuard will be exactly as invisible as these products were unless discoverability is treated as a first-class problem. As opportunity: the vocabulary is uncaptured. Nobody owns plain-language framings like "delete your auth code," "one place for who-can-do-what," or "tenant tree as the source of truth" — the words a bleeding developer actually types. The companies above compete for searchers who already know the jargon; the open position is being findable by the person ForgeGuard's founder was two projects ago.

## Consequences for the brief

Three amendments follow. First, the differentiation section should be rewritten from "no such place exists" to the composition claim above, naming Frontegg and Permit.io as the near-neighbors and the model-plus-sovereignty combination as the moat. Second, the MVP validation gains a second question beyond "would I hand-roll again?": *knowing Cerbos and Permit exist, would I still build this?* — the founder should actually spend a day each with Cerbos and Permit's free tiers before committing months, because a teardown from marketing pages is weaker evidence than contact with the products. Third, positioning and discoverability enter the plan as engineering-equal concerns: the category's jargon wall means ForgeGuard's early adopters will be found through plain-language pain framing, worked examples, and open-source distribution — not through competing for the word "auth" or the acronym soup.
