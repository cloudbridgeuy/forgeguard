# ForgeGuard — Report 1: Lessons Learned from the Field

*Version 1.0 — July 2026*
*Companion to the Problem & Product Brief and the Competitive Teardown. Purpose: extract what the competitors learned the hard way — from their engineering blogs, docs, and practitioner post-mortems — and decide what each lesson changes in ForgeGuard's design. Each section ends with a concrete amendment or a deliberate rejection.*

---

## 1. The New Enemy Problem will find you, and ForgeGuard's monotonic graph only half-dodges it

The single most valuable engineering artifact in this space is the consistency model Google published in the Zanzibar paper and SpiceDB refined. The failure mode has a name — the New Enemy Problem — and a canonical shape: permissions are cached for performance, a grant is revoked, and for a window of staleness the revoked user still passes checks. SpiceDB's answer is ZedTokens: opaque tokens representing a point-in-time of the datastore, letting every request choose its own consistency — `minimize_latency` for cache-friendly reads, `at_least_as_fresh(token)` when the check must reflect a known write, `fully_consistent` as an expensive last resort. The deeper insight from their engineering writing: strong consistency is only *needed in select circumstances*, so the right design is ad-hoc, per-request consistency selection rather than one global setting.

Now map this onto ForgeGuard honestly. The brief's permit-only monotonic graph means *adding* access is always safe under staleness — a stale cache can only under-grant, never over-grant, for additions. But the two mutation types that remain are exactly the dangerous ones: *removing* a grant (the fired-contractor case) and denies. This is not a corner case; revocation is the entire reason the New Enemy Problem has "enemy" in its name.

**Fold in:** three design commitments, since elevated into a full consistency section of the brief. First, denies bypass caching entirely — they are evaluated first, the set is tiny by design, and a strongly consistent read of a near-empty list is cheap. This turns the circuit-breaker design into a consistency guarantee: a deny takes effect *now*, everywhere (while connected), which is precisely what the 2am incident demands and becomes a marketable property ("revocation latency: one request"). Second, grant *removals* propagate through the synchronization contract's event stream, which doubles as the cache-invalidation bus; the gap-free sequence number is a consistency token in the Zanzibar sense and is public API from day one — mutations return the resulting revision, requests may demand minimum freshness, and applications may store revisions alongside resources. Third, every decision evaluates against a single grant-store revision (never mixed reads) and the decision log records snapshot version plus grant revision, making every historical decision replayable *and* freshness-auditable. Degraded operation — an enforcement point that cannot refresh — is integrator-configured with a chosen default of serve-last-known-state, paired with loud staleness signaling; the full reasoning lives in the brief's consistency section.

## 2. The list-filtering problem is the pure-proxy model's biggest hole — and the fix is already in your architecture

Every check-API product hits the same wall, and the practitioner literature is unanimous about it: authorizing `GET /invoices/123` is easy; authorizing `GET /invoices` — return *only* the rows this principal may see — is an order of magnitude harder. Policy engines verify inputs but don't index data, so naive filtering means one check per row. The field's answers: Oso's Local Authorization compiles policy into SQL fragments your own PostgreSQL executes; Cerbos's Query Plan API partially evaluates the policy and returns the residual conditions as a filter; a Red Hat team using SpiceDB with Quarkus resorted to streaming responses just to hide the per-item check latency. One production architecture document (an ADR for an APISIX + OPA gateway) describes exactly ForgeGuard's proposed shape — edge enforcement with a header contract — and names collection endpoints as the case the gateway alone cannot solve.

Candidly: this is the strongest technical objection to ForgeGuard's proxy-first model, and the brief currently doesn't address it. An edge enforcement point can decide *whether* you may call the list endpoint; it cannot, by itself, decide *which rows* come back.

**Fold in:** two moves, one immediate and one deferred. Immediate: reframe the RLS layer from the startup's war story — currently cast as part of the fragmentation disease — as ForgeGuard's *data-plane partner*. The signed scope header (`X-Fg-Scope` carrying the org-subtree FGRN) is exactly what a downstream service sets as a database session context that generic, ForgeGuard-shipped data-layer filter policies consume. The proxy and RLS stop being two drifting systems and become one system with two enforcement altitudes, driven by the same spine. This costs almost nothing in the MVP (the middleware sets the session context) and honestly answers the collection-endpoint question on day one. Deferred: a query-plan-style API — "give me the residual filter for principal P, action A, resource type T" — belongs on the roadmap, not in the MVP; Cerbos took multiple release cycles to stabilize theirs, and partial-evaluation techniques (which Cedar, among others, demonstrates) are the known implementation family whichever engine an engineering design selects.

## 3. The dual-write problem — which ForgeGuard's model designs away, with one condition and three residues

Practitioners consistently report that synchronizing authorization data with application data is the dominant cost of adopting any external authorizer — one hands-on comparison puts data synchronization at half to two-thirds of total implementation effort, and AuthZed's own literature dedicates serious attention to the dual-write problem: your app creates a resource in its database and must also register it with the authorizer, and those two writes can't be atomic across systems. The standard mitigation is the transactional outbox pattern.

The lesson for ForgeGuard turned out to be the opposite of the obvious one. Under the cardinality doctrine (section 4), the problem the outbox exists to solve largely does not arise. Ordinary resource creation is one row insert in the app's own database, inside the app's own transaction, with an anchor FGRN the app already holds from the signed header — ForgeGuard never learns the resource exists, so there is nothing to keep in sync. The exceptional path — sharing — is likewise a *single* write, to ForgeGuard alone, on one condition that must be promoted to a rule in the brief: **a promoted resource's FGRN is derived from the application's native identifier** (shape: `fgrn:{org}:resource:{type}/{native_id}`), so promotion requires no app-side column, migration, or mapping write. If resource FGRNs were opaque minted identifiers instead, the app would have to store the mapping and the dual write would return through the side door.

Three residues remain, and none is the classic dual-write — but the correct response to them is the opposite of casual. Handling them lazily would reproduce, inside ForgeGuard's own integration story, the "precarious" quality the brief diagnoses in hand-rolled systems. They are therefore served by one first-class **synchronization contract** (now specified in the brief's data-plane section): a durable, per-Organization event log with gap-free sequence numbers, delivered by signed webhooks with at-least-once delivery, idempotent application, and cursor-based replayability — the log is the primitive, push is a delivery mode, and pull via cursor polling reads the same truth. The position dimension table becomes an ordinary consumer of that stream. Dangling promotions are collected by design: an idempotent tombstone API (fire-and-forget safe) plus a reconciliation endpoint an SDK-shipped reconciler diffs against. Principal provisioning becomes an idempotent, natively-keyed upsert API inbound and lifecycle events outbound. SpiceDB's Watch API is the precedent worth studying for the streaming half.

**Fold in:** the FGRN-derivation rule enters the brief's naming section as a requirement, not a suggestion. The synchronization contract enters the brief as first-class MVP surface — with the honest scope boundary that the *log and cursor endpoint* are the MVP primitive and push webhooks follow shortly after. The outbox pattern disappears from the guidance entirely: idempotent, replayable APIs make it unnecessary, which is itself the marketable property — the model dissolves the category's most expensive adoption problem, and the sync contract makes the residue boring. Both halves belong in positioning.

## 4. WorkOS's high-cardinality doctrine independently confirms the spine — adopt it explicitly

WorkOS's re-architected FGA ships with an unusually opinionated piece of guidance: the authorization store is optimized for low-cardinality entities (workspaces, accounts, apps), and high-volume objects — documents, rows, messages — should *stay in the application's database*, carrying only a reference to their nearest managed parent; checks pass that parent, and permissions evaluate through the hierarchy. They explicitly argue that syncing every object into an external store creates latency and reconciliation problems, and that local-evaluation approaches couple authorization to your production schema.

This is ForgeGuard's spine model arrived at independently, and it resolves a question the brief left implicit: what, exactly, gets a node in the DAG?

**Fold in:** a stated cardinality doctrine. Org units, principals, principal-sets, and *shared or exceptional* resources are DAG citizens with FGRNs. The bulk of application resources are not — they live in the app's database with a parent FGRN column, and the enforcement decision uses the parent (which the header contract already carries). A resource is *promoted* into the DAG only when it needs individual grant edges — the moment someone shares it. This keeps the graph small, the checks fast, and the dual-write surface (section 3) minimal, and it gives the sharing model a crisp trigger: sharing is what mints a resource FGRN.

## 5. Shadow mode is the adoption feature — an enforcement point nobody trusts yet must be able to watch before it acts

A recurring migration pattern in the Zanzibar ecosystem: run the new authorizer in parallel with the legacy checks, let the old system decide, and log discrepancies until the new model provably matches — OpenFGA's contextual tuples (passing hypothetical relationships in a check without persisting them) exist substantially to support this kind of validation. For a product whose pitch is "put me in the hot path of every request and delete your auth code," the scariest moment in the customer journey is turning it on.

**Fold in (adopted into the brief, v1.4):** observe-only mode is an MVP feature, not a later convenience. The middleware and proxy both support a per-endpoint `enforce | observe` switch; in observe mode every request flows through, decisions are logged as *would-allow/would-deny with reasons*, and nothing blocks. The migration story writes itself: install, observe, review the would-deny report, flip to enforce endpoint by endpoint. Contextual/hypothetical evaluation is the same machinery as "view as," delegation evaluation, and policy testing — one investment, several payoffs — and the brief now states this sharing explicitly.

## 6. Developer experience is where the open-source winners actually compete

The tooling bar in this category is high and visible: SpiceDB ships a CLI, a playground, and annotated documentation; OpenFGA has a VS Code extension, a local test CLI, and a visual playground; Cerbos treats policy tests in CI as a core workflow and publishes guidance on validating configuration in pipelines; Cedar ships a formatter, a language server, and — uniquely — a symbolic compiler that formally verifies policy properties and produces concrete counterexamples. These are small companies spending scarce engineering on *policy ergonomics*, because that's what converts developers.

**Fold in (deliberately deferred):** the founder's decision is that developer experience grows organically during the MVP rather than entering the brief as specified surface — the toolchain will be shaped by the friction actually encountered while living with the product, which is truer to the n=1 validation method than speccing tools in advance. The lesson stays recorded here as the bar the field sets: a validator, a colocated test harness runnable in CI, and a local dev mode are what competitors consider table stakes, and compile-time authorization linting (deny shadowing, unreachable endpoints, empty selectors — the symbolic-analysis idea Cedar demonstrates) remains the unshipped differentiator to revisit once the MVP shows where the real friction is.

## 7. Pricing lessons: the market repriced downward, and free tiers now carry no feature gates

Permit.io publicly restructured pricing twice in two years, landing on a free tier to 1,000 MAUs with an explicit "no blackout features" policy — every feature available at every tier, pay only for quota — plus a ~$150/month startup tier; WorkOS gives away ten million authorization checks a month. The direction of travel is unambiguous: generous free tiers with full feature access are now table stakes, and gating *features* (rather than scale) reads as hostage-taking in this category.

**Fold in (adopted, then generalized):** the hosted low tier must be feature-complete, differentiated only by quota — capability gating contradicts the exit-hatch promise. And since the brief went technology-neutral, pricing acquired a second life there: because final pricing depends on the architecture chosen, the brief now converts the demand-side numbers into an **architecture fitness criteria** section — measurable unit-economics, latency, consistency, and scale requirements any candidate design must demonstrate, each with a stated breaking point and re-evaluation triggers. Pricing stopped being a page in the brief and became the acceptance test for architectures.

## 8. Agent delegation chains are where the category's demand is moving — the header contract should be born ready

Every competitor pivoted its messaging to AI-agent authorization in 2025–2026, but underneath the marketing there is a real modeling requirement: an agent acting on behalf of a user must carry *both* identities, with the effective permission being an intersection — the agent's own scope narrowed by the delegating user's rights. WorkOS models agents as separate credentialed identities; Permit's gateway "interrogates" agents and carries context downstream; Cerbos gates which tools an agent may invoke per the human behind it.

**Fold in (adopted into the brief, v1.4):** agents, and agents-on-behalf-of-users, are native principal kinds from the beginning. Principals come in three kinds — humans, services, agents — and a delegation chain is itself a first-class principal whose effective scope is the intersection of its members' rights, evaluated and logged with the full chain visible. Not agent features, just the shape — and the brief now states that "view as" is a self-delegation with narrowed scope, making the general case and the existing primitive one mechanism.

## What was deliberately *not* folded in

For the record, three things the field does that ForgeGuard should resist. Building a visual policy playground before the MVP validates (tooling gravity is real; the winners built engines first). Supporting multiple policy engines à la Permit's mix-and-match (optionality tax on a one-person team; the model is the product, not engine pluralism). And chasing the AI-agent marketing wave with dedicated product surface now — the delegation *primitive* above is cheap and structural; an "agent security platform" is a repositioning that the teardown showed is already the noisiest, most crowded message in the category.
